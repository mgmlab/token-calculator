Workshop playbook: where should your AI workloads run?

A facilitator’s guide for a 90-minute working session with a customer. Together we describe their workload in the calculator, test the constraints that matter to them, and leave with a shared view of the economics: buy servers, rent GPUs, pay per token, or a mix, and what would change the answer.

This is a workshop, not a demo. The customer’s numbers drive the session, not ours. The calculator is the shared whiteboard: we fill it in with them, change it with them and read it with them. Pellera isn’t trying to prove that on-prem wins; we’re working out where each workload belongs. The result is an economic view under stated assumptions, not a quote and not an architecture decision on its own: security, model quality, latency, data residency, resiliency and operations can override cost.

Figures in the reference patterns were taken from the live calculator on October 3, 2026. API and GPU rental prices refresh daily, so dollar figures drift; the calculator re-checks every pattern’s result each day and flags any that change. Server prices are placeholders until we have quotes, which is why every pattern shows Confidence: Low.

What the customer leaves with

  1. A written model of their workload: users, usage, speed, peak and constraints, agreed by the people who own them.
  2. The economic answer for that workload, with the cost of every option side by side and a confidence rating.
  3. What would change the answer: the two or three assumptions or constraints that decide it.
  4. Next steps with owners: usually vendor quotes, a benchmark in the Pellera lab, or a pilot.

Who should be in the room

Customer
  • Application or product owner (who uses it and how)
  • Infrastructure or platform lead (where it would run)
  • Security or compliance (which providers and data rules apply)
  • Finance or procurement, for the last 20 minutes if possible
Pellera
  • Facilitator: runs the conversation and keeps time
  • Architect: drives the calculator and explains the math
  • Note-taker: fills in the worksheet below

Before the workshop

  1. Send the discovery questions a week ahead. In the calculator, open the Input guide and use Copy questions. Ask for real numbers where they exist: pilot usage, logs, licence counts, the approved-provider list, data rules.
  2. Pick one reference pattern close to their situation (see below) and open it once to know today’s figures.
  3. Start clean. Click New analysis and tick “clear my Data editor changes”, so a previous customer’s prices or exclusions don’t carry over.
  4. Check the header. “Prices checked today” should be green. If it mentions an example to review, a pattern’s result has changed; check it first.
  5. Bring quotes if you have them. A real server quote or the customer’s contracted cloud rate can be entered in the Data editor during the session.

For a 60-minute session, shorten blocks 2 and 4 to five minutes each. For a deeper session, run blocks 3 to 5 once per workload.

1 Frame the decision 10 min

Ask: “Which AI workloads are you deciding about, and what would a good decision look like a year from now?”

Lay out the four ways to run a model: buy servers, rent GPUs in a cloud, pay per token to an API provider, or a hybrid of owned servers plus an API for the overflow. The honest answer depends on how many people use it, how much text goes in and out, how fast it must be, how busy the peak is, and who they are allowed to buy from.

Capture: the workloads in scope, and the decision criteria (cost, control, data residency, speed to start).

2 Calibrate with a reference pattern 10 min

Open the one pattern closest to them (see Using the reference patterns for which to pick, or Examples ▾ in the calculator). Show the Analysis summary, then pull its what-if so the room sees the answer move.

Ask: “Which parts of this look like you, and which don’t?” Their answer is the bridge into block 3.

3 Build their workload 30 min

Click New analysis and name the scenario after their workload. Work through the inputs in order, using each input’s ? help and the Input guide for the question to ask and typical values. Let the customer give the numbers; where they don’t know, agree a range and start in the middle.

  • Users: active users, not licences.
  • Requests per user per day: count model calls, not clicks (agents and search often make several calls per action).
  • Input and output size: how much text goes in (including documents and history) and comes out.
  • When it runs: active days per month and the busy-hour share.
  • Model: the model they would actually use, and whether a smaller one is good enough (a question to test, not assume).

Capture: every input on the worksheet, with who supplied it and how sure they are.

4 Stress-test the answer 20 min

Now change what matters to them. Each question below maps to a setting; change it live and read the summary again. Two or three of these usually decide the answer.

Capture: which constraints are firm, and which what-ifs changed the answer.

5 Read the result together 15 min

  • The Recommended box: the lowest-cost option; its big number matches that option’s row in the tables.
  • Click a box to open the row behind it and highlight the cost lines that add up to it; ↑ Back to summary returns.
  • Click the range (the small circled i) to show what is still an estimate (the speed estimate and power load) and how far it could move the cost. Placeholder server prices are not varied: they are used as entered and flagged ⚠ until replaced with a quote.
  • Confidence: High means nothing marked ⚠, Medium one, Low two or more. Name what would raise it: a quote, a lab benchmark, reviewed operating assumptions.
  • The Breakeven tab: the table shows the lowest-cost option at ¼× to 50× today’s usage, which turns “buy or not” into a planning conversation.

Ask: “Does this match your intuition? If not, which assumption feels wrong?”

6 Agree next steps 5 min

  • Which numbers still need confirming, and who owns each one.
  • What would raise confidence: vendor quotes for the leading server option, a benchmark in the Pellera lab, the contracted cloud or API rate.
  • Whether a pilot makes sense, and on which option.

Capture: owners and dates on the worksheet.

Using the reference patterns

The seven patterns below are worked examples of realistic situations, each landing on a different result. In a workshop they are a calibration tool, not the content: they show the room how the answer moves, so the customer trusts it when we build their own numbers. Most workshops use one, occasionally two.

When to use one
  • Block 2, to calibrate (5–10 minutes): after the customer has said what they are deciding, before we enter their numbers.
  • Block 4, to make a constraint concrete: when they can’t yet put a number on something, show its effect on a pattern (“here is what limiting providers did for a similar rollout”).
  • When the customer has no data yet: start from the nearest pattern and adjust its inputs together instead of a blank form.
  • Internal practice: run all seven before your first workshop to learn how each lever behaves.
When not to
  • Don’t open with a pattern before the customer has framed their own decision.
  • Don’t walk through more than two; it turns the workshop back into a demo.
  • Never present a pattern’s numbers as the customer’s answer. They are illustrative; their own scenario replaces them.

How to run one (five minutes)

  1. Open it with Open in the calculator (or Examples ▾). The note above the summary says what drives the result.
  2. Read the Analysis summary aloud: the Recommended box and the one-line why.
  3. Pull its one what-if (the setting names in the Try line are links to the field) and let the room watch the answer change.
  4. Ask “what’s different about you?”, then click New analysis and build their workload.

PATTERN C

24/7 contact center, 2,000 agents

Strong on-prem candidate
Open in the calculator ↗

Use it when the customer looks like: a customer-facing service that runs every day and most hours (contact center, support, operations).

Preset Contact center AI, 2,000 agents, 60 requests each a day, every day of the month, approved providers only. About 10 billion tokens a month.

Buy servers$59K/yrOne 8× L40S server, right-sized, 23% utilized
Rent GPUs$126K/yrNebius, on-demand
Pay per token$85K/yrAzure AI Foundry
Hybridnot neededOne server already covers the busiest hour
What decides it
  • Steady, round-the-clock demand keeps owned GPUs busy, which is what makes owning pay.
  • One right-sized 8× L40S server costs about $26K a year less than paying per token. Buying for the full calculated peak would cost about $66K; the extra $7K buys capacity for a few busy minutes a day.
  • In the busiest hour about 6% of requests wait, and 95% start within a second. A cloud fallback would cost about $45 a year in tokens, so adding one is a resilience choice, not a cost one.

Takeaway for the room: the shape of demand, not just its size, decides where a workload belongs.

Ask the customer

Does traffic really run all day, every day, or mostly in business hours? How long could a customer wait for a response at the busiest moment?

What-if to try together

Change Active days per month to 21.7 and Busy-hour share to 15% (business hours only).

The result flips to API candidate: utilization drops to about 12%, and paying per token (about $61K/yr) is cheaper again.

Questions you’ll get

“Confidence says Low. Should we trust this?”

The rating counts the inputs still marked ⚠: none is High, one is Medium, two or more is Low. Today the three that can move it are throughput (no measured benchmark yet), server pricing (no quote yet) and the default operating assumptions. Low means the direction is preliminary, not wrong. Click the range under the Buy servers box to show which inputs are still estimates and how far they move the cost; a real quote and a Pellera lab benchmark turn it into something fit for an architecture decision.

“We get discounts from our cloud provider.”

We start from public pricing so every option has the same baseline. Enter the contracted rate or a real infrastructure quote in the Data editor during the session and see whether the answer changes.

“Why not just use GPT or Claude?”

Those are different models, shown for cost context only. Set Closed-model tier to Frontier and the Breakeven tab shows what a flagship model would cost (Pattern B: about $645K/yr against $115K for the hybrid). Whether a smaller open model is good enough is a question to test together, not assume.

“How current are these prices?”

API and GPU rental prices refresh every day from public sources; hover “Prices checked” in the header. Server prices are updated by hand from quotes.

After the workshop (within two working days). Send the PowerPoint export (summary, confidence, every option, the breakeven table and the assumptions), the Excel workbook for the leading option (expand its row → Export this option) for their engineers, and the completed worksheet with owners and dates. Keep each workload’s share link for the Pellera team so we can reopen the analysis; don’t send it to the customer, since the calculator isn’t shared with customers for now. Follow up on quotes and lab benchmarks so the next conversation starts at Medium or High confidence.
